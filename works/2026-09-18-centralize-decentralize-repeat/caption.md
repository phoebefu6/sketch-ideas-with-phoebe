Your data team reorg has a birth certificate. It is dated 1960.

I kept hearing the same sentence this year - "we're centralising the data team again" - and I
wanted to know whether the pendulum people keep referring to is a real pattern or just something
we say. So I went looking for the first person who wrote it down.

It is Douglas McGregor, in The Human Side of Enterprise, 1960. He calls it the accordion effect.
A big move toward decentralisation happens, the consequences take their toll, top management
decides things have gotten out of hand and tightens up, and then the tightened thing cannot be run
centrally, so it loosens again. He was writing about factories. There were no data teams.

So I drew every turn since, on a true time axis, and let the shape do the talking.

The glass house, one machine and one queue. VisiCalc in 1979 and Lotus 1-2-3 in 1983, which nobody
called a decentralisation programme but which put analysis on every desk. Inmon's one enterprise
warehouse in 1992, Kimball's marts in 1996. Hadoop in 2006 and the data lake named in 2010 - keep
everything, decide later. Redshift and Snowflake pulling it all back into one store. Data mesh in
2019. And now a central platform with federated governance, an AI Act that recentralises policy,
and domain ownership that is not going back.

Six reversals. And the turns keep getting closer: fifteen years from the glass house to the
spreadsheet, four from the cloud warehouse to the data mesh.

Here is what changed for me. I used to argue about which model was right. Looking at the shape, I
do not think that is the question. Both have been right, briefly, six times, because each turn is
a correct local response to what the previous one broke. Centralising buys one definition of
revenue and a queue. Decentralising buys speed and eleven definitions of revenue. Neither failure
is fixed by swinging - it is fixed by deliberately paying for the thing your current half is bad
at, while you are in it.

So the questions I would actually ask, if I joined a team mid-swing: which half was I hired into,
what did the last turn break that nobody has budgeted to fix, and which of the things we are
building will still be true after the next one.

One thing I deliberately did not draw: a prediction. The last stretch of the line is pale and
dashed and has no arrowhead, because the current turn is not finished and I have no idea where it
lands. Every date on the sheet is published and sourced; the vertical position of each event is my
editorial reading, and the work says so.
